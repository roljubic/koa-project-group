O'ahu Contractor Contracts: What Every Owner Needs to Review Before Signing

The contract you sign before a renovation begins determines almost everything that happens after. Most owners sign whatever the contractor puts in front of them. Here's what to look for — and what to demand — before you pick up that pen.

Why Contractor Contracts Matter More in Hawaii

On O'ahu, renovation projects operate in a unique environment that amplifies the consequences of a poorly written contract. Material costs are 30–60% higher than the mainland. Skilled trade labor is scarce. The permit process through the Honolulu Department of Planning and Permitting (DPP) can add months to a timeline. And the pool of licensed, reputable contractors is smaller than most owners expect.

When something goes wrong — and on a complex renovation, something always goes wrong — the contract is the document that determines whether you have recourse or not. An owner with a well-drafted contract can enforce a schedule, dispute a change order, withhold retainage for defective work, and require lien waivers from subcontractors. An owner with a poorly drafted contract — or no written contract at all — has almost no leverage.

This guide covers every contract clause that matters for O'ahu renovation projects, from mandatory Hawaii law requirements to the nuanced protections that separate owners who get what they paid for from those who don't.

Required Contract Elements Under Hawaii Law

Hawaii Revised Statutes §444-25.5 requires written contracts for all home improvement work exceeding $1,000. The contract must include:

  • Contractor's full legal name and business address
  • Hawaii contractor license number — verify at pvl.ehawaii.gov
  • Project description — what work is being done
  • Total contract price
  • Payment schedule — amounts and timing
  • Start date and estimated completion date
  • Notice of cancellation rights — homeowners have 3 business days to cancel a home improvement contract signed at their home

A contractor who refuses to provide a written contract for work over $1,000 is violating Hawaii law and should be disqualified immediately. There is no legitimate reason for any professional contractor to work without a written agreement.

Payment Schedule: What's Fair and What Isn't

The payment schedule is where most owners make their biggest mistake — paying too much too early, leaving themselves without financial leverage when problems arise.

The Standard O'ahu Renovation Payment Structure

Milestone % of Contract
Contract signing / mobilization10–15%
Demolition complete / site cleared10–15%
Framing complete15–20%
Rough-in complete (plumbing, electrical, HVAC)15–20%
Drywall / finish surfaces complete15–20%
Substantial completion10–15%
Final completion (punch list done, permits closed)10% (retainage)

The 10% Retainage Rule

Retainage is a percentage withheld from each payment until final completion. It is your most powerful tool for ensuring the contractor finishes every last item. Without retainage, contractors are 100% paid before the punch list is complete — and the phone stops getting answered.

Always include 10% retainage. Release it only when: final inspection is passed, all permits are closed by DPP, and the punch list is fully completed to your satisfaction.

Red Zone: When to Walk Away on Payment Terms

  • Contractor requests more than 30% upfront → significant red flag
  • Contractor requests 50%+ before any work begins → walk away
  • No payment schedule tied to milestones → walk away
  • Contractor refuses retainage provision → significant concern

Scope of Work: The Devil in the Details

The scope of work section is where renovation disputes are born. Vague scope language is the contractor's friend and the owner's nightmare.

What Specific Scope Looks Like

Vague (dangerous): "Kitchen remodel including new cabinets, countertops, and appliances."

Specific (protected): "Kitchen remodel including: removal and disposal of existing cabinets and countertops; installation of 42 linear feet of semi-custom shaker cabinets (specific manufacturer and model); 28 sq ft of quartz countertops (specific stone, color, edge profile); installation of owner-supplied appliances (4 items listed); new under-cabinet LED lighting; new plumbing fixtures (specific manufacturer); tile backsplash (specific tile, 18 sq ft); painting of kitchen walls and ceiling (specific paint color)."

Every item in the scope should specify: what product is being installed, the brand/model/specification, who supplies it (contractor-supplied vs. owner-supplied), and the extent of work (sq footage, linear feet, unit count).

Exclusions Are as Important as Inclusions

Explicitly list what is NOT included. Common O'ahu renovation disputes arise from assumptions about:

  • Asbestos or lead paint remediation (common in pre-1980s Hawaii homes)
  • Subfloor repair discovered after flooring removal
  • Termite damage (drywood termites are endemic to O'ahu)
  • Electrical panel upgrade (often required for kitchen/bathroom remodels)
  • Permit fees — confirm whether included in the contract price

A well-drafted contract specifies an "allowance" for potential unknowns (e.g., "$3,000 allowance for subfloor repairs") rather than leaving them as open-ended surprises.

Change Orders: Your Most Important Protection

Change orders are written amendments to the original contract for work that was not in the original scope. They are how legitimate additional costs get documented — and how illegitimate costs get introduced if you're not careful.

Change Order Requirements

Every change order should include:

  • Description of the additional or changed work
  • Fixed price (not "time and materials" if you can avoid it)
  • Schedule impact — does this add days to the completion date?
  • Signatures of both parties before work begins

Critical rule: Never authorize work verbally. In Hawaii, verbal change orders are not binding. If a contractor starts additional work without a signed change order, you are not legally obligated to pay for it — though disputes will follow. The only safe approach: no signature, no start.

Protecting Against Change Order Abuse

Some contractors intentionally underbid the base contract, then make their profit on change orders. Signs of this pattern:

  • Bid is significantly lower than all other bids
  • Change orders start appearing within the first two weeks
  • Change orders are vaguely described with round-number pricing
  • Contractor resists your requests for itemized change order pricing

An owner's representative reviews all change orders before you sign them — assessing whether the scope is legitimate, the pricing is reasonable, and the schedule impact is accurate. Schedule and Delay Clauses

Schedule provisions are notoriously weak in standard contractor agreements. Contractors rarely agree to hard completion deadlines with financial penalties — and on O'ahu, where permit delays and material shipping add genuine uncertainty, some schedule flexibility is reasonable.

What to Include

  • Scheduled start date: Specific date, not "approximately" or "as soon as permits are issued"
  • Substantial completion date: The date by which the property is habitable / the primary work is done
  • Final completion date: Punch list and permits closed
  • Excused delays: List specifically — permit delays beyond contractor control, material shortages, weather (O'ahu's Kona storms can halt exterior work)
  • Notice requirement: Contractor must notify you in writing within 5 business days of any delay

Liquidated Damages (Use Carefully)

Liquidated damages clauses charge the contractor a daily fee for each day past the scheduled completion. They're difficult to enforce in Hawaii and often cause contractors to walk off jobs rather than pay them. A better approach: tie final payment and retainage release directly to completion — no completion, no final payment.

Lien Waivers: The Protection Most Owners Miss

Hawaii mechanics lien law (HRS Chapter 507) allows subcontractors and material suppliers to place a lien on your property for unpaid amounts — even if you paid your general contractor. If your GC doesn't pay their electrician, that electrician can lien your property for their unpaid amount.

How to Protect Yourself

At each payment milestone, require your GC to provide:

  1. Conditional lien waiver from the GC — waives their lien rights upon receipt of the current payment
  2. Unconditional lien waivers from major subcontractors — waives their rights for work through the prior payment period

Do not release payment without receiving these waivers. A GC who resists providing lien waivers is a significant red flag — either they're not paying their subs, or they're unfamiliar with standard practices.

Insurance and License Requirements

Before signing any contract, verify:

License Verification

  • Search the Hawaii DCCA Professional and Vocational Licensing database: pvl.ehawaii.gov
  • Confirm: license is active, license type matches the work being done, no disciplinary actions
  • General Contractor license (B) is required for most whole-project renovations over $1,000
  • Specialty contractors (electrical C-13, plumbing C-37, HVAC C-52) need their own specialty licenses

Insurance Requirements

Require certificates of insurance before work begins. Minimum for O'ahu renovation projects:

  • General Liability: $1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate
  • Workers' Compensation: Hawaii state-required limits (all employees)
  • You as additional insured: Your name must appear on the GL certificate as additional insured

An unlicensed contractor is uninsurable. Any work done by an unlicensed contractor voids your homeowner's insurance coverage for that work and may void your policy entirely.

Warranty and Defect Provisions

Hawaii law provides a minimum implied warranty on new home construction, but renovation work is less clearly covered. Specify warranty terms explicitly in your contract.

Standard Warranty Language

Contractor warrants all work to be free from defects in workmanship for a period of [1 year] from the date of substantial completion. Materials warranties pass through from manufacturer. Contractor will repair or replace defective work at no cost to owner during the warranty period.

O'ahu-Specific Warranty Considerations

  • Roof work: Minimum 2-year workmanship warranty — O'ahu's rain and UV conditions accelerate failure of improperly installed roofing
  • Waterproofing: 5-year warranty is appropriate for shower pans, lanai waterproofing, and exterior cladding
  • Paint: 2-year warranty — salt air and UV degrade improperly prepared or applied exterior paint rapidly
  • HVAC: Manufacturer warranty only (1–10 years depending on equipment); confirm contractor registers equipment for full warranty

Contract Red Flags: When to Walk Away

  • 🚩 No written contract for work over $1,000 — illegal in Hawaii, walk away
  • 🚩 No license number listed in the contract — verify before signing
  • 🚩 50%+ upfront payment required — contractor is cash-poor or will disappear
  • 🚩 Vague scope of work — "kitchen remodel" with no specifications is an invitation to dispute
  • 🚩 No change order clause — verbal approvals only is a trap
  • 🚩 No lien waiver provision — exposes you to subcontractor liens
  • 🚩 No insurance certificate before work begins — you assume all liability
  • 🚩 "We've always done it this way" response to any of these requests — professional contractors welcome reasonable contract provisions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a standard payment schedule for an O'ahu renovation contract?

10–15% at signing, milestone-based progress payments, and 10% retainage held until final completion. Never pay more than 25–30% upfront or more than 50% before substantial completion.

Does a Hawaii contractor contract need to be in writing?

Yes — Hawaii law requires written contracts for home improvement work over $1,000 (HRS §444-25.5). A verbal agreement for renovation work over $1,000 is unenforceable in Hawaii.

What is retainage and should I include it in my O'ahu renovation contract?

Retainage (typically 10%) is withheld from each payment until final completion. It's your strongest tool for ensuring punch list completion. Always include it; release only after permits are closed and all work is done.

Can an O'ahu contractor file a lien on my property?

Yes — under HRS Chapter 507, even subcontractors and suppliers your GC didn't pay can lien your property. Require lien waivers from your GC and major subs at each payment milestone.

What should I do if my O'ahu contractor is asking for changes mid-project?

All changes must be in a written, signed Change Order before work begins. No exceptions. Verbal approvals are not binding in Hawaii and create the conditions for end-of-project surprise invoices.
